# Hens party ideas

Hens, bridal showers, and the quiet version

There is no standard hens party. Some people want a weekend in a winery town with fourteen friends; plenty want six people, a long lunch and home by four. Both are correct. Here's the full range, a checklist with real deadlines, and how to handle the money without ambushing anyone.

## Start with three questions

Ask the bride these before you plan a single thing, and take the answers literally:

- **What's the budget ceiling — for you, and for your friends?** This is the question that decides everything else. Some of the guest list is already paying for a wedding outfit, travel and a gift.
- **Who's on the list, and who absolutely isn't?** The bride approves the guest list. Aunties, future mothers-in-law and work friends are all fine, but not as a surprise.
- **Surprise, or not?** Ask outright. A lot of people genuinely hate being surprised, and you can't tell from the outside.

Then decide who is actually running it. One organiser, one money person (ideally not the same overwhelmed maid of honour), and a shared list so everyone can see what's covered.

## At home

Cheapest, most flexible, and often the one people remember. No minimum spend, no time limit, and anyone can duck out early.

- Long lunch or a grazing table — everyone brings a component.
- Cocktail or mocktail making, with one person on the shaker and a printed recipe card each.
- A craft afternoon: candle making, flower crowns, pottery painting kits, embroidery, a sewing-machine disaster.
- Spa night — face masks, nails, a stack of towels, someone's good speaker.
- High tea at home. Scones, sandwiches, a borrowed tiered stand, real champagne glasses.
- A film night with the bride's worst favourite movie and proper snacks.
- Backyard barbecue, lawn games, an esky, no schedule at all.
- A recipe shower — everyone brings a handwritten recipe card and the dish to go with it.

## A day out

- High tea at a hotel, or a long lunch somewhere with a set menu (set menus make the bill splitting easy).
- Winery, brewery or distillery tour with a designated driver or a minibus.
- Cooking class — pasta, dumplings, Thai, cake decorating.
- Pottery wheel throwing, life drawing, terrarium building, perfume blending, a painting-and-drinks studio.
- Day spa, or a bathhouse afternoon.
- Picnic in a botanic garden with a hamper and a rug per pair.
- Beach day, harbour or river cruise, a hired boat.
- Mini golf, ten pin, roller skating, karaoke booth, a comedy show.
- Gallery or market crawl, then dinner.

## A weekend away

The biggest commitment and the one most likely to cause quiet financial stress. It works when it's booked early, costed honestly, and offers a day-only option for people who can't do the whole thing.

- A wine region within a couple of hours of home — the Hunter, the Barossa, Mornington Peninsula, Margaret River, the Yarra Valley.
- A big house rental with a pool, and one nice dinner cooked at home.
- Coastal: Byron, Noosa, the Great Ocean Road, Rottnest, Port Douglas.
- Country and spa towns — Daylesford, the Blue Mountains, Bright.
- A city weekend: hotel, one show, one long dinner, one lazy brunch.
- Camping or a farmstay, if that's the crowd — vastly cheaper and usually funnier.

## Activity-led

Good for a group that doesn't all know each other — a shared task beats forced small talk, and nobody has to drink to have something to do.

- Surf lesson, stand-up paddleboarding, kayaking.
- Dance class — salsa, heels, burlesque, a routine for the reception.
- Indoor climbing, tennis, a social touch-footy game, a hike with a swim at the end.
- Escape room, trivia night, a scavenger hunt around town.
- Horse riding, archery, go-karting.
- A volunteering morning, if that's her — some people would genuinely rather plant trees than hold a plastic flute.

## Genuinely quiet options

Worth saying plainly: a low-key hens is not a failed hens. If she's an introvert, unwell, pregnant, broke, grieving, or just tired of events, plan smaller and better.

- Six people, one long lunch, no agenda.
- A walk, a swim, and fish and chips.
- A day spa for four and dinner afterwards.
- Breakfast — cheap, short, and over by eleven.
- A gallery, a bookshop, a good coffee, home by three.
- A pyjama night with takeaway and the phones in a bowl.
- No hens at all, and a nice dinner with her partner's people instead.

## The planning checklist

Rough timings for a hens with a weekend or a booked venue. For a lunch at someone's house, compress the whole thing into three weeks.

1. **Four to six months out** Talk to the bride: budget ceiling, guest list, surprise or not, and any dates that are already gone. Confirm who's organising and who's holding the money.
2. **Three to four months out** Lock the date with the bride and the key people, then book accommodation and anything that sells out — restaurants, classes, minibuses. Pay deposits and note exactly what's refundable.
3. **Eight to ten weeks out** Send the invitation with the total cost estimate, what it includes, the dress code and an RSVP date. Nobody should ever learn the price after they've said yes.
4. **Six weeks out** Collect the first payment — usually the accommodation or deposit share. Chase once, politely, then follow up individually.
5. **Four weeks out** Confirm numbers with the venue, book transport, buy decorations and prizes, and start the who's-bringing-what list so nobody buys three lots of balloons.
6. **Two weeks out** Final payment, final numbers, and every dietary requirement and allergy passed to the venue in writing. Confirm every booking by phone.
7. **One week out** Send the run sheet: times, addresses, what to wear, what to bring, the wet-weather plan, and one contact number that isn't the bride's.
8. **The day before** Pack, charge everything, print anything printed, put the games in one bag, and text the group the meeting point and time one last time.
9. **On the day** Assign someone to photos and someone to timekeeping so it isn't all on you, and leave gaps in the schedule. An over-programmed day is the most common way a good plan goes wrong.

## Money, without ambushing anyone

This is where hens parties go bad. It's almost never the amount — it's finding out the amount too late to say no.

- **Publish the number before the RSVP.** "About $180 all up: $90 accommodation, $55 dinner, $35 activity" lets people decide with dignity. A vague "shouldn't be much" always ends badly.
- **Split by what each person actually does.** Someone who comes to lunch but skips the dinner shouldn't pay for the dinner. Itemise: beds per bed, activities per head, food kitty per person per day.
- **Offer a day-only tier** on any weekend away. It rescues the guests with kids, shift work, or no spare money, and they'll be grateful for years.
- **The bride usually pays nothing** — but conventions vary by family and friendship group. Some groups cover her entirely, some cover her meals and activities only, some split everything evenly because that's what she'd want. Decide it early and say it out loud rather than assuming.
- **One kitty holder, one account, one deadline.** Post a simple running total — what came in, what's been paid out — so nobody has to ask.
- **Set the deadline before your own money is at risk.** Don't personally float four thousand dollars of accommodation and hope.
- **Give people a graceful exit.** Say, once and in writing, that anyone who can't make the numbers work should just say so and come to the free part. Then never mention it again.

## The bride who hates surprises

Roughly half of them do, and the ones who do *really* do. The fix isn't to abandon surprise entirely, it's to be strategic about what stays secret.

- **Tell her the frame:** the date, the rough cost, the dress code, the physical demands, whether alcohol is involved, and who's coming. Nobody has ever been delighted by a surprise abseil.
- **Keep the details secret:** the venue, the theme, the cake, the playlist, who's flown in, what her sister has planned.
- **Anxiety-proof it.** Give her a schedule with a clear end time and a way to leave. "Home by 4pm" is a gift.
- **Check the medical and personal stuff privately** — pregnancy, sobriety, injuries, a family member who shouldn't be in the same room as another. This is exactly why the bride approves the list.

## Games that don't humiliate anyone

The good ones are about the couple, not about embarrassing the bride in front of strangers. Skip anything involving dares with members of the public, drinking penalties, or props she'd be mortified to be photographed with. Ten minutes of a game is plenty; nobody wants an activities schedule.

- **Mr & Mrs / How well do you know them:** record the partner answering questions beforehand, then see if she matches. Reliably the best twenty minutes of any hens.
- **Advice cards:** everyone writes one line of marriage advice; read them out or bundle them into a book she keeps.
- **Date night jar:** each guest writes a date idea on a card. Genuinely used afterwards, unlike most hens crafts.
- **Photo timeline:** everyone brings one photo of themselves with the bride; lay them out in order and let her tell the stories.
- **Two truths and a lie, about the bride** — teams guess. Works well when the room doesn't all know each other.
- **Wedding-day predictions:** sealed envelopes with guesses — who cries first, length of the speeches — opened at the anniversary.
- **Recipe cards:** each guest brings a handwritten recipe for the couple's kitchen.
- **Guess the year:** baby photos, old hairstyles, terrible formal outfits. Affectionate, not cruel.
- **A kind scavenger hunt:** find a dog to pat, a stranger willing to give marriage advice, the best flat white in three blocks.

## Making it work for everyone in the room

- Have real non-alcoholic options, not just soda water — someone may be pregnant, sober, driving, or simply not drinking, and shouldn't have to explain it.
- Check accessibility before booking: stairs, standing time, walking distance, the accessible bathroom, how far the car park is.
- Feed people properly. Most hens party regrets start with three hours of drinking and a bowl of chips.
- If a guest is breastfeeding or bringing a baby, tell them the plan early so they can work out what's possible.
- Mixed generations often work best in segments — afternoon tea with the aunties, dinner with the friends.
- Name badges are not embarrassing when half the room has never met.
- Confirm how everyone's getting home before anyone starts drinking.

## FAQ

### How far in advance should you plan a hens party?

Four to six months for a weekend away or anything needing accommodation, six to eight weeks for a day out with a booked venue, and about three weeks for a lunch or a night at someone's house. Send the invitation with the cost eight to ten weeks out so people can plan.

### Who pays for the hens party?

Conventions vary. Most commonly guests pay their own way and split the bride's share between them, but some groups split everything evenly and some cover only her meals and activities. Agree it early, say it out loud, and put the number in the invitation.

### How do you split the cost fairly?

Itemise it — beds per bed, activities per head, food kitty per person per day — and charge people only for the parts they attend. Offer a day-only option for anyone who can't do the whole thing, and set one payment deadline with one person collecting.

### What if the bride doesn't want a big party?

Then don't throw one. A long lunch with six people, a spa day for four, or a walk and fish and chips are all legitimate hens parties. Ask her directly and take the answer at face value.

### Should a hens party be a surprise?

Only if she likes surprises — ask her. A good compromise is to tell her the date, the cost, the dress code, the physical demands and who's coming, while keeping the venue, theme and details secret.

### What's the difference between a hens party and a bridal shower?

A bridal shower is usually a daytime gathering with gifts, often hosted by family and including older relatives. A hens party is typically friends only, later in the day, and gift-free. Plenty of people now do one combined event, which is fine — just be clear on the invitation which one it is.
