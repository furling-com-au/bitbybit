# Meal train ideas

For the week someone can't cook

Somebody's had a baby, come home from surgery, or lost someone. You've said you'll cook. Here's what actually helps once it lands on their bench — which is a slightly different question from what tastes good.

## The short version

The best meal train dish is one they can ignore. It goes in the freezer, it comes out three weeks later on the night everything falls apart, and it doesn't need a decision, a shopping trip, or a clean saucepan. Five things make a meal good:

- **It freezes and reheats without going strange.** They may not want dinner on the night you deliver it. That's fine — the freezer is the actual gift.
- **It comes in a container nobody has to return.** Foil tray, takeaway container, ice-cream tub. A returned dish is a chore disguised as a kindness.
- **It's labelled.** What it is, when it was made, what's in it, and how to heat it.
- **It's portioned for one meal at a time**, not one giant block of lasagne.
- **It arrives without requiring a conversation.** Text first, leave it at the door, go home.

## The label does half the work

Write it on masking tape on the lid, or on a card taped to the top. This is the whole thing, and it takes forty seconds:

CHICKEN & VEG PIE — from Sam (0400 000 000)

Made Tue 12 March. Best by 12 June.

Contains: wheat, dairy, egg.

From frozen: 190°C, 45 min, foil on for the first 25.

Thawed: 190°C, 25 min. Heat until steaming right through.

Serves 4. Tray is disposable — no need to return.

The allergen line matters most. The reheating line is second — a sleep-deprived person standing at an oven at 6pm should not have to guess a temperature. Add "no need to return" even if you don't mean it; you can always take the dish back another time.

## Meals that freeze and come back well

- Lasagne, pastitsio, baked ziti — the classics for a reason.
- Cottage pie or shepherd's pie in a foil tray.
- Bolognese, ragu or a big chilli, frozen flat in bags. Pasta goes mushy, so send the sauce and a packet of dry pasta.
- Curries: butter chicken, korma, rogan josh, massaman, dhal, chana masala. Nearly all improve after a freeze.
- Soups that are already a meal — minestrone, pumpkin, chicken and barley, pea and ham, congee.
- Casseroles and stews: beef and red wine, chicken and leek, sausage and lentil, osso buco.
- Meatballs in sauce, rissoles, san choy bau mince.
- Pies and pasties, sausage rolls, spanakopita — freeze unbaked or baked, both work.
- Enchiladas, burrito filling, pulled pork or beef for rolls.
- Fried rice and biryani — better than you'd expect.

Freeze it properly.

Cool it fast (spread it out, or
 sit the container in cold water) before it goes in — a warm dish in a full
 freezer warms everything around it. Freeze flat in bags where you can; it
 thaws in a fraction of the time and stacks like books.

## Things that don't freeze well

Cream and yoghurt sauces split. Potato in chunks goes grainy — mashed on top is fine, diced in a stew is not. Plain rice and pasta turn to paste; send them separate and dry. Salad leaves, fresh herbs, crumbed and fried things, boiled eggs, and anything with a crisp topping all lose. Nothing here is a disaster — just send those parts fresh, or on the side.

## Meals that travel hot and hold

If you're delivering dinner for tonight, pick something that doesn't mind a twenty-minute drive and an hour on the bench:

- A roast chicken with a tray of vegetables.
- A tray bake — chicken thighs, chorizo, potatoes, capsicum.
- A big pot of soup with a good loaf of bread.
- Baked pasta, delivered in the tray it was cooked in.
- A curry with rice in a separate container.
- Honestly: a roast chook from the supermarket, a bagged salad kit and a tub of good potato salad is a completely respectable dinner and nobody will think less of you.

In summer, use a cool bag and don't leave anything on a doorstep in the sun. The Australian rule of thumb for food between 5°C and 60°C is two hours to get it back in the fridge, four hours total before it's binned. Text on arrival so it goes inside.

## Portions for a household that can't cook

Ask how many people and whether kids are eating the same thing. Then:

- **Cook your normal recipe and a half**, and split it into two or three containers rather than one. Two two-serve containers are far more useful than one four-serve block, because they can defrost dinner for tonight without committing to leftovers for three days.
- **Include the sides.** A curry without rice is homework. Add the rice, the bread, the salad, the sour cream, the coriander.
- **Add one extra meal's worth** for the freezer. Week two is when the casseroles stop and the exhaustion doesn't.
- For a single person, small containers are the whole point — nobody recovering from surgery wants to eat the same lasagne six nights running.

If several of you are cooking, spacing matters as much as quantity. Every second or third day beats four dinners on Monday and nothing after Thursday. ## Breakfast and snacks are underrated

Almost everyone brings dinner. Almost nobody brings breakfast, and breakfast is the meal that gets skipped in a hard week. A box of these is as welcome as any casserole:

- Overnight oats or bircher in individual jars.
- Egg and vegetable muffins, or a frittata cut into squares.
- Banana bread, savoury scones, a tray of muesli slice.
- Good bread, butter, jam, a decent block of cheese.
- Cut fruit in a container — washed, chopped, ready. Enormously more likely to be eaten than a bag of whole apples.
- Boiled eggs, hummus, cheese sticks, nuts, crackers, yoghurt.
- Coffee, tea, cordial, electrolyte sachets, a slab of sparkling water.

For a household with a newborn, the test is whether it can be eaten with one hand, standing up, while holding a baby. Nearly everything above passes. A hot meal that needs cutlery and two hands often doesn't.

## New parents, specifically

Ask, don't assume. But some things are near-universally welcome: food that reheats in a microwave in three minutes, one-handed snacks by the feeding chair, a lot of water and easy drinks, and fruit and vegetables in a form that requires no preparation. Fresh, simple and fibre-rich tends to be appreciated in the early weeks. If the parent is breastfeeding, ask whether anything has been ruled out — some babies react to things in the parent's diet and the family will know what they're avoiding.

Keep visits short or skip them entirely. "I'm leaving it on the step at 5, no need to come out" is one of the kindest sentences you can send.

## After surgery, specifically

Appetite is often small and unreliable, and there may be genuine medical restrictions — soft foods after dental or throat surgery, low fibre or low fat after some abdominal operations, reduced salt or fluid limits for others. **Ask what they've been told they can eat, and follow it exactly.** Don't improvise around a discharge sheet.

Where there are no restrictions, aim gentle: soups and broths, congee, mashed potato, scrambled eggs, poached chicken, steamed fish, risotto, yoghurt, custard, smoothies. Small portions in small containers, so an untouched meal isn't a whole dinner wasted. Send a couple of easy things alongside the real meal — sometimes toast and soup is all that gets eaten.

## After a loss

Numbers are unpredictable — relatives arrive, then everyone leaves at once and the house is silent. Freezable food is the answer, because the hard weeks come after the casseroles stop. Send things that can feed six or one. Include breakfast, snacks and something sweet. And keep going in a month, when the meal train has finished and everyone else has moved on.

## Dietary needs are a safety issue

Treat this as the non-negotiable part. Coeliac disease, nut allergy, shellfish allergy and severe dairy allergy are medical conditions, not preferences, and "there's only a bit in it" is not a plan. So:

- **Ask up front**, before you shop — allergies, religious requirements, vegetarian or vegan, textures they can't manage, and anything they simply can't face.
- **Label every allergen** on the container, every time, even when you're confident.
- **Watch the hidden ones**: soy sauce contains wheat, most stock cubes and gravies contain gluten, Worcestershire sauce contains fish, pesto usually contains nuts and parmesan, and many curry pastes contain shrimp.
- **If it's a serious allergy, clean surfaces and use clean utensils**, or say honestly that you can't guarantee a nut-free kitchen and bring something shop-bought with a full ingredient list instead. That's the responsible answer, not a failure.

## Drop-off, done well

- Text on the way, and again when you're at the door.
- Leave it on the step unless they've said to come in. Assume they're asleep, feeding, or crying.
- Don't stay. Don't ask to hold the baby. Don't need a cup of tea.
- Don't expect a thank-you text, and say so: "no need to reply."
- Esky, cooler bag or a box on hot days. Nothing sitting in the sun.
- Never bring something that needs to go straight in the oven at the exact time you arrive.

## Help that isn't food

Half of what a struggling household needs isn't dinner. Offer something specific, with a time attached — "I'll take the bins out Tuesday nights" beats "let me know if you need anything", which puts the work of asking onto the person with none left.

- A milk, bread and fruit top-up, dropped at the door.
- The school run, or an afternoon with the older kids.
- A load of washing, hung out and brought back folded.
- Walking the dog. Feeding the cat. Mowing.
- An hour holding the baby so they can shower and sleep.
- Driving to appointments, or sitting in the waiting room.
- Paying for a cleaner once, or a delivery gift card for the nights nobody cooks.
- A text in six weeks, when everyone else has stopped checking.

## FAQ

### What should I cook for a new mum?

Something freezable and reheatable, portioned small, plus one-handed snacks and breakfast food. Lasagne, curry, soup, cottage pie and pasta sauce all work; add cut fruit, muesli slice, boiled eggs and plenty of drinks. Ask about allergies and anything they're avoiding before you shop.

### How much food should one meal train meal be?

Cook your usual recipe and a half, then split it into two or three containers rather than one large one. Include the sides — rice, bread, salad — and add an extra portion for the freezer.

### What should I avoid bringing?

Cream-based sauces, chunky potato and plain rice or pasta if it's going in the freezer; anything needing assembly or precise timing; dishes you want returned; and anything at all that conflicts with a dietary restriction or post-surgery instruction.

### Should I use disposable containers?

Yes. Foil trays, takeaway containers and ice-cream tubs mean nobody has to wash, store and return your dish. Write "no need to return" on the lid.

### What do I bring for someone recovering from surgery?

Ask what they've been told they can eat and follow it exactly. Without restrictions, gentle small portions work best: soups, congee, mashed potato, poached chicken, risotto, custard and smoothies.

### How often should meals be delivered?

Every second or third day is usually better than daily — most meals make leftovers, and freezer space runs out. Spread the roster so support continues into week three and four, not just the first week.
