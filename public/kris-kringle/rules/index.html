Secret Santa, the Australian way

Kris Kringle rules

Kris Kringle is what most of Australia calls Secret Santa — same game, different name, usually shortened to KK. Here's how to run one that doesn't end with someone unwrapping a novelty mug in total silence.

The rules, in one paragraph

Everyone who's in puts their name in. Each person is randomly assigned one other person, secretly, and buys them one gift up to an agreed limit. Nobody draws themselves, nobody tells, and everyone unwraps at the same moment. That's the whole game.

Two things are worth settling up front. Mutual pairs: is it fine if Sam draws Priya and Priya draws Sam? Most groups don't mind; some prefer one unbroken chain so it can't happen. And group size — below about five people the secret doesn't survive, because in a group of three everyone works out the whole draw over lunch.

The budget, and how to make it stick

Australian workplaces most often land between $10 and $30, with $20 and $25 doing most of the work. Families who use KK to replace buying for eleven cousins usually go higher — $50 is common there, because it's replacing five gifts, not one. Nothing is standard; what matters is that everyone hears the same number.

  • Say one number, not a range. "$20 to $30" becomes an arms race: one person spends $30, everyone else feels like they've turned up light.
  • Say whether it's a cap or a target. "Up to $20" and "around $20" produce visibly different piles. Pick the wording and repeat it.
  • Say the floor out loud too. "$20 is the ceiling, not the goal." A $7 thing chosen well beats a $20 thing chosen at the servo at 8:40am, and people need permission to spend less.
  • Include postage if anyone in the group is remote — otherwise the interstate person is quietly $12 down.
  • Don't pool the money. Everyone buys their own gift. The moment one person is holding everyone's cash, you've invented admin.

Timing

Draw the names at least two weeks before the swap, and three to four if anything is being posted. December post is slow and a gift that arrives on the 27th isn't a gift, it's a parcel.

In offices, the practical window is a draw in mid-to-late November for a swap at the Christmas lunch in the first half of December — the back end of December is leave, and half the team won't be there. Families can draw any time from October in the group chat, or in person at whatever gathering already exists.

Decide the swap moment before you draw: the actual date, time and place. "At the lunch" is not a plan when three people are remote. If anything is being posted, name a posting deadline a week earlier. Three reminders is the sweet spot — at the draw, a fortnight out, three days out.

The office rules that prevent disasters

Almost every KK horror story is one of the same six mistakes. Put these in the note with the budget and you'll skip all of them.

  • Nothing that needs a size. Clothing, shoes, rings. You will guess wrong, and the guess becomes the story.
  • Nothing personal. Skincare, perfume, anything for the body. It implies you've been paying a kind of attention nobody asked for.
  • Nothing that reads as feedback. Gym gear, diet anything, a book about being organised for the person who's always late. Funny in your head, pointed in the room.
  • Mind the scent, and check on alcohol. Candles and diffusers are a migraine in an open-plan office; plenty of workplaces have a booze policy, and plenty of people don't drink for reasons they don't owe anyone.
  • Nothing that creates admin. Subscriptions they have to activate with their own card, apps that need a sign-up, plants that need watering by someone who travels.
The one-sentence version of every workplace gift policy: would this be fine opened in front of their manager and their nan at the same time? If you hesitate, buy the coffee beans.

Joke gifts, and when they land badly

Joke gifts are fine when the joke is about a shared situation — the printer, the coffee machine, the car park, the client everyone's sick of. They go wrong when the joke is about the person: their age, their body, their relationship status, their driving, the thing that happened in March.

The test: the joke should be funniest to the person unwrapping it. If it's funniest to the room, it isn't a gift, it's a bit — and they have to stand there holding it while everyone watches. Safest structure is a real gift plus a small joke rather than a joke instead of one; someone who spent $20 and received a rubber chicken is entitled to be quietly filthy about it. If your group genuinely wants a gag round, make it the whole rule — "everything from an op shop, $10 max" — so nobody is left holding the only novelty in the room.

People who join late, leave, or drop out

This is the most common thing that breaks a draw, and it has tidy fixes.

  • Someone joins after the draw. If nobody has bought anything yet, just re-draw — it's the cleanest option and takes seconds if your draw is digital. If shopping has started, splice them in: pick one existing pair (say Priya was buying for Tom), and change it so Priya buys for the newcomer and the newcomer buys for Tom. One person gets a quiet message; nobody else is affected.
  • Someone leaves. Close the loop. If Alex was buying for Sam, and Jordan was buying for Alex, then Jordan now buys for Sam. One change, no re-draw, no announcement.
  • Set the in-or-out rule early. "If you're in the draw you buy one and you get one. Pull out before the deadline and you're out of both." Stated up front, it never has to be argued.
  • Someone forgets. Every year. The organiser's job isn't to shame anyone, it's to know by the day before — a quiet "bought / not yet" check three days out gives you time to have a spare on hand.

And the kindest rule of all: nobody unwraps until everyone has something in front of them.

Gift ideas that work between $10 and $30

Categories rather than brands, because the good version of each of these exists near you and in your price band.

  • The thing they use daily and won't replace. The frayed charging cable, the dying earbud tips, a pen worth keeping, a bottle opener that isn't a fridge magnet. Highest hit rate on this list.
  • One good consumable instead of a multipack. A single bag of beans from a roaster, a jar of proper chilli oil, good honey, decent olive oil. The $9 block of chocolate beats the box of six that costs $9.
  • Summer, because it is. An Australian December is beach weather: zinc, a hat that packs flat, barbecue tongs, a cooler that isn't tacky, a picnic rug at the top of the budget.
  • Desk things that aren't novelties. A plant that survives neglect, a mug a grown adult would choose, a small card game that travels from the desk to the pub.
  • Local and specific beats generic. A $20 voucher for the good coffee place downstairs is a recommendation as well as a gift. A generic gift card is just money with extra steps.
  • The consumable inside a hobby you know about. If they fish, garden, brew, run or sew, buy the thing they run out of rather than the thing they'd choose. Nearly foolproof.

Drew someone you barely know? Ask whoever sits nearest them one question — "what's always on their desk?" — and buy the better version of that.

Kris Kringle, Secret Santa, gift exchange

Three names for the same draw. Kris Kringle is what most Australians call it, Secret Santa is the usual name almost everywhere else, and an office running it formally will often call it a gift exchange. Same rules: everyone draws one name, keeps it quiet, and buys for that person only.

No emails, on purpose

Most Secret Santa sites want everyone's email address before a single name is drawn. Then the invites bounce, or land in spam, or sit unopened next to the electricity bill, and by December someone's chasing Nan for a login. One link in a chat that everyone already reads skips all of it. Works in WhatsApp, Messenger, Teams, the family thread — anywhere a link can go.

Wishlists fix most of this

Telling people what you'd like isn't unromantic; it's the entire point of a group that has agreed to buy each other presents. Three lines is plenty: one thing you'd genuinely use, one thing to avoid, and a size if a size could ever matter. It's far easier to write when it's anonymous — nobody knows who's reading it.

FAQ

Is Kris Kringle the same as Secret Santa?

Yes. Kris Kringle — often just "KK" — is the usual name in Australia and Ireland for what Americans call Secret Santa. Same rules: names drawn secretly, one gift each, an agreed budget.

What's a normal Kris Kringle budget?

Australian workplaces usually settle between $10 and $30, with $20 and $25 the most common. Families who use KK instead of buying for everyone often go to $50. There's no standard — pick one number, say whether it's a cap or a target, and tell everyone the same thing.

When should we draw the names?

At least two weeks before the swap, and three to four if gifts are being posted. Offices generally draw in mid-to-late November for a swap at the Christmas lunch in early December, since the last fortnight of December is mostly leave.

What if someone joins after the draw?

If nobody has bought yet, re-draw — it's cleanest. If shopping has started, splice them in: take one existing pair and redirect it, so the giver now buys for the newcomer and the newcomer buys for the original recipient. Only one person needs to be told.

What if someone leaves before the swap?

Close the loop. Whoever was buying for the person who left now buys for the person that leaver was buying for. One change, no re-draw needed.

Are joke gifts okay?

Only when the joke is about a shared situation rather than the person, and best paired with a real gift rather than replacing one. If the joke is funnier to the room than to the person unwrapping it, don't.

Should you tell people what you want?

Yes. A short wishlist — one thing you'd use, one thing to avoid, a size if relevant — is the single biggest improvement you can make to a Kris Kringle, and it's easiest when the wishlist is anonymous.
