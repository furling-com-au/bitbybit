# Group gift ideas, by occasion

When everyone chips in

The whole point of a group gift is that pooled money buys something one person couldn't. Get that right and it's the best gift they'll get; get it wrong and it's a $400 gift card in a nice envelope.

## The rule that makes group gifts work

A group gift should be something **no single person in the group could reasonably have bought alone**. If eighteen people pool $25 and buy a slightly nicer version of a normal present, the group added nothing except a longer card.

So spend the pot on depth, not breadth. One expensive right thing. One weekend booked and paid for. One job they've been putting off, done. The test to apply to any idea: *would this still be a good gift if only one person's name were on it?* If yes, it's probably too small for a group.

## How much to ask per person

Naming an amount feels rude and not naming one feels vague, so most organisers hedge and end up with a pot they can't plan around. The fix is to name a number *and* visibly lower the stakes in the same sentence:

"We're getting Mum something decent for her 60th.

$25 each

gets us to about $300 and I've got a plan for it.
 Anything at all is welcome though — chip in what suits, I'm not keeping a
 list."

That does three jobs: it gives people a default so they don't have to guess, it makes less than the default explicitly fine, and it promises nobody will be audited. Then hold to it — **never publish who gave what**, and put every name on the card regardless of amount.

Set the number by what the lowest-paid person in the group can shrug at, not what the highest-paid person thinks is reasonable. In an office that usually means casuals, juniors and part-timers set the ceiling. And give a deadline ("by Friday") rather than chasing people, because chasing is what makes group gifts feel like a levy.

Roughly what different bands buy you:

| Per person | 5 people | 8 | 12 | 20 | 30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10 | $50 | $80 | $120 | $200 | $300 |
| $20 | $100 | $160 | $240 | $400 | $600 |
| $30 | $150 | $240 | $360 | $600 | $900 |
| $50 | $250 | $400 | $600 | $1,000 | $1,500 |

Assume one or two people won't chip in, and plan against about 85% of the top number.

## Deciding as a group without a forty-message thread

Group gifts rarely fail for lack of ideas. They fail at the decision.

- **Never open with "any ideas?"** An open question in a group chat gets three suggestions and silence. Open with two or three concrete options and ask people to pick.
- **Gather, then vote, then one person buys.** Committees can choose; they cannot purchase.
- **Give silence a meaning.** "If I don't hear anything by Thursday, I'm booking the first one."
- **Ask the person closest to them for the veto list, not the idea list.** Partners and adult kids are terrible at "what should we get?" and excellent at "they already have one" and "they'd hate that".
- **Watch for flattening.** A group converging on the least objectionable option always lands on a voucher. That's not agreement, it's exhaustion.

## The gift card default — and when it's actually right

Vouchers get a bad name mostly because of how they're arrived at: not chosen, just settled for. But there are real cases where a voucher is the *best* gift — someone moving overseas who can't take objects, someone renovating who'd rather choose the tap themselves, or a colleague you genuinely don't know well enough to buy for.

If it's a voucher, make it a chosen one. Pick a specific shop rather than a generic card, so it reads as a recommendation. Say in the message what you hoped they'd spend it on. And attach one small physical thing to open — an envelope alone gives them nothing to do with their hands in front of everyone.

## Milestone birthdays — 30th, 40th, 50th, 60th and up

The shape of a good milestone gift shifts with age. At 30 and 40 the upgrade lands well: the proper version of a thing they use constantly and have been making do with. From 50 onwards most people are actively trying to own *less*, and gifts that skew towards experiences, time and things that get used up do far better than objects.

**What to get someone for their 60th**, in rough order of how well they tend to land:

- A weekend away, or a night somewhere genuinely good, with the travel covered as well as the room.
- A photographer for an hour with the whole family. Most people over 60 have no recent picture of everyone together, and it's the gift that keeps being mentioned years later.
- The serious version of the hobby tool they've been making do with — real secateurs, a proper bike service and helmet, a decent telescope, the good mixer.
- A job they keep putting off: the garden bed built, the trees pruned, the deck oiled, a cleaner for a month.
- A course, membership or season ticket for the thing they said they'd try when they had time — or something commissioned, like an illustration of the house they raised everyone in.
- Something collected from everyone — a [recipe book](https://bitibybit.com/recipe-collection/), a book of letters, a photo book. Best value per dollar of anything on this page.

What to avoid: anything with the number 60 printed on it, a framed collage they now have to find a wall for, and a bottle so expensive they'll feel obliged never to open it.

## Retirement

The brief is usually already written: it's the thing they've said "one day" about for years. Money towards a named trip, the good version of the hobby they're retiring into, a membership somewhere they'll go weekly, a course, a proper pair of binoculars.

The traditional watch or engraved plaque lands well for some people and cold for others; the deciding factor is whether the job was the identity, so ask someone who'd know before defaulting to it. Either way, add the part money can't buy: written contributions from people they worked with, collected quietly. That's the item most retirees actually keep.

## Farewell — leaving a job or the city

Smaller budget, shorter runway, and one constraint: they may have to carry it. Aim portable.

- Something from the place they're leaving — the local roaster, the bakery, a print by someone local. It becomes a souvenir of the city rather than of the office.
- A voucher for where they're going — genuinely useful in the first fortnight somewhere new — or a good travel item if the move is a big one.
- A card everyone actually wrote in. It sounds like the small part of the gift; it's the part that gets kept.

Avoid heavy, fragile, or branded with the company logo.

## New baby

This is where pooling pays for itself, because the alternative is eight sets of 0000 onesies arriving the same week.

- Put the pot towards **the expensive item the parents chose** — pram, car seat, carrier. Ask them; never guess on anything safety-related.
- Buy for six to twelve months rather than newborn. Everyone buys newborn.
- Buy time: a cleaner for a month, a nappy service, or food. A [meal train](https://bitibybit.com/meal-train/) beats almost any object in the first six weeks, and costs the group very little each.
- Something for the parent, not the baby. It is startling how rarely anyone does this.

## Weddings

Pooled cash is normal and often expected — the trick is making it not feel like a bank transfer. Name what it's for. A group can fund a specific part of the honeymoon, cover the one registry item nobody could manage alone, or commission something for the new place. ## Housewarming

New homeowners are rich in cushions and poor in boring expensive things. A group is perfectly placed to buy the unglamorous stuff nobody gifts: a decent ladder, a real toolkit, a fire blanket and extinguisher, a wheelbarrow, hose fittings that don't leak, a load of soil and mulch delivered. At the top of a pot, a tradesperson's visit for the job they've already started dreading. Resist decorating for them — you don't know the palette and neither do they yet. And on moving night, dinner delivered beats anything wrapped.

## "We've all chipped in and have no idea"

Three moves work almost every time:

- **Upgrade the daily thing.** Whatever they use every day and complain about — the pillow, the pan, the chair, the headphones.
- **Buy time.** A cleaner, a gardener, a month of meals, a hand with the job they're dreading. Nobody has ever resented this.
- **Buy the collected thing.** Letters, recipes, photos from everyone. It costs almost nothing and outlasts the rest.

Still stuck? Split the pot: one shared experience with everyone in it, plus one small physical thing to open on the day. And the escape hatch that isn't shameful — just ask them. "We've pooled some money and we'd rather get you the right thing than surprise you" is a sentence most people are relieved to hear.

## FAQ

### How much should each person chip in for a group gift?

Name one default amount and make less explicitly fine. In workplaces $10 to $30 a head is the usual band; among close family or friends $50 and up is common. Set the number by what the lowest-paid person can shrug at, give a deadline rather than chasing, and never publish who gave what.

### What do you get someone for their 60th?

Experiences and time beat objects at that age. A weekend away with travel covered, a family photo session, the serious version of a hobby tool they've made do with, a job around the house done for them, or something collected from everyone like a recipe book or a book of letters. Avoid anything with "60" printed on it.

### How do you decide on a group gift without endless discussion?

Don't open with "any ideas?" — open with two or three concrete options. Gather, vote, then hand the buying to one person. Set a deadline and say that silence means yes. Ask the person closest to the recipient for the veto list rather than the idea list.

### Is a gift card an acceptable group gift?

Sometimes it's the best gift — for someone moving overseas, renovating, or a colleague you don't know well. It fails when it's what the group settled for rather than chose. Make it a specific shop instead of a generic card, say in the message what you hoped they'd use it on, and attach one small thing to open.

### What's a good office gift for someone leaving?

Something portable and from the place they're leaving — a local roaster, bakery or artist — a voucher for where they're going, and a card everyone genuinely wrote in. Avoid heavy or fragile items if they're flying, and anything with the company logo on it.

### What should a group get new parents?

Put the pot towards the expensive item they chose themselves, like the pram or car seat, and never guess on safety gear. Buy for six to twelve months rather than newborn, and consider buying time instead: a cleaner, a nappy service, or a run of meals in the first six weeks.
