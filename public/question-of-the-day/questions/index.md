## Why the habit dies

It's almost never the questions. It's that one person becomes responsible for producing a good one before 9am, every single day, forever. That person gets busy, misses a Tuesday, feels a bit sheepish on the Wednesday, and by Friday nobody mentions it again. Three failure modes cover nearly all of it:

- **The unpaid second job.** Thinking of a fresh question daily is a real task that appears on nobody's list and gets no credit. It is the first thing dropped in a busy week.
- **The same six voices.** If answering means speaking up in a meeting, you hear from the same confident handful and everyone else waits it out. That's not engagement, that's an audience.
- **Questions that cost something to answer.** "What's your biggest fear?" and "What's your proudest moment?" ask for disclosure at work, to colleagues, on a Tuesday. People deflect, and the deflecting is what makes it feel cringe.

The fix for all three is the same shape: make the question arrive by itself, make answering a single tap rather than a performance, and keep the stakes at pineapple-on-pizza level. Nobody has ever felt exposed by admitting they're a thick-spread Vegemite person.

## Questions that start an argument

These are the ones worth saving for a flat Wednesday. They split a room roughly down the middle, and the split never lands where anyone expects. Two people will get genuinely heated about the straw. That's the point.

- Is a **hot dog** a sandwich?
- Does a straw have **one hole or two**?
- Is **cereal** a soup?
- Is **water** wet?
- Toilet roll: **over or under**?
- Milk first or cereal first?
- Does the week start on **Monday or Sunday**?
- Is the letter H said **aitch or haitch**?
- Is a **tomato** a fruit or a vegetable?
- Is a **doughnut** a cake or a bread?
- Is **silence** a sound?
- Is a **hallway** a room?
- Is an **egg** breakfast food or anytime food?
- Does pressing a lift button twice make it arrive faster?
- In a band name, is "The" part of the name?
- Do you **say** the alphabet or **sing** it?
- Is a chair with only one leg still a chair?
- If a turtle loses its shell, is it **naked or homeless**?
- Is a **rubber duck** a toy or a colleague?
- Is a **piano** a percussion instrument or a string instrument?

## The Australian ones

Reliable in an Australian office, and unexpectedly good with a team split across states — the potato scallop question is a genuine map of the country.

- Is it a **potato scallop** or a **potato cake**?
- Vegemite on toast: **thin scrape** or **thick spread**?
- Is the **ibis** a bin chicken or a magnificent bird?
- Is a **sausage roll** a type of pie?
- Sauce on a meat pie: **essential or optional**?
- Hot chips: **tomato sauce or gravy**?
- Is a **jaffle** a toastie, or its own thing entirely?
- Tim Tam Slam: with **tea** or with **hot chocolate**?
- Fairy bread: sprinkles **edge to edge** or **a bit sparse**?
- Is the **magpie warble** the best bird sound going, or wildly overrated?
- Sand at the beach: **half the fun**, or **a menace** you find for weeks?
- Perfect Saturday: **beach or bush**?
- Which is the best bit of the week: **Friday arvo** or **Saturday morning**?
- Always find the **good shopping trolley**, or always find a **park right out the front**?

## What to avoid

A daily question is a low-stakes ritual, not a values exercise. The ones that go wrong nearly always ask for something a person can't take back:

- **Anything that surfaces money, health, family status or religion.** "Where are you really from", "when are you having kids", "what did you weigh" — even asked warmly, these land differently depending on who's in the room and who your manager is.
- **Ranking colleagues.** Who's funniest, who'd survive the apocalypse, who'd you pick first. Someone is always last.
- **Anything that requires a story.** "Most embarrassing moment" is a performance request, not a question, and it disproportionately costs the quietest person in the room.
- **Anything that only lands for one group.** A question about a TV show, a sport, or a school system splits the room into people who get it and people waiting for it to end.

The test is simple: could the newest person in the team answer it truthfully in one word, on their second day, in front of their manager? If not, save it.

## How to run it so it lasts

- **Take it off a human.** The question should already be there when people arrive, not waiting on someone's Tuesday. That single change is the difference between a habit and a chore.
- **One tap, not a round-the-room.** A vote means everyone participates, including the people who'd never volunteer an answer out loud — and the split is more interesting than any one person's answer.
- **Hide the tally until they've voted.** Show a running score and people follow the crowd. The whole fun of "is a hot dog a sandwich" is finding out afterwards that the office is 60/40.
- **Keep it to ninety seconds.** The moment it needs an agenda item, it's finished.
- **Let it be asynchronous.** Post the link once. People tap it with their coffee, or at 9pm, or not at all. Nothing is scheduled and nobody is put on the spot.

## Common questions

### What is a good question of the day for work?

One that everyone can answer in a single word without revealing anything about themselves — a would-you-rather, a food argument, or a straight preference. "Is a hot dog a sandwich" works in every workplace. "What's your biggest regret" does not.

### How do you run a daily question with a remote team?

Asynchronously, with a link rather than a meeting. Share one link that shows today's question and takes a tap to answer, then let people hit it whenever their day starts. Nobody has to be online at the same time, and people in other time zones aren't answering yesterday's question.

### How long should a question of the day take?

Ninety seconds if it's in a stand-up, or no scheduled time at all if people answer it on their own. Once it needs its own agenda item it has stopped being a warm-up and started being a meeting.

### Should you show the results before people vote?

No. Visible tallies pull people towards the leading answer, and the interesting part is the honest split. Hide the numbers until someone has picked a side, then show them what the room actually thinks.

### What if someone doesn't want to answer?

Let them skip it, silently and without follow-up. A question of the day that tracks who hasn't participated has turned a bit of fun into attendance-taking, which is the fastest way to make people resent it.
