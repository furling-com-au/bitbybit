# Bring a plate ideas

Australian for "bring food to share"

First, the thing half the internet is actually here for: a plate means food on it. Then the useful part — what to bring when there's no oven, when it has to survive a hot car, when it has to feed twenty, and when you have forty minutes and a supermarket.

## What "bring a plate" means

**It means bring a dish of food to share with everyone.** Not an empty plate. Not a plate for yourself. Not a place setting. It's an Australian and New Zealand invitation convention — descended from country hall suppers and the old wording "ladies a plate" — and it catches out new arrivals every single year. Somebody always turns up at a school fundraiser holding a clean dinner plate, and somebody always tells that story for the next decade.

What you bring is up to you unless the invitation says otherwise. The unspoken expectation is one dish, enough for roughly eight to ten people as part of a spread, ready to eat when it arrives. If the host has run a signup list, follow it — that list exists because last time there were six pavlovas and no salad.

## How much is "a plate"?

Bring enough that ten people can help themselves without it looking picked over. Nobody counts, but everybody notices. These are catering rules of thumb, per person, assuming your dish is one of several on the table:

| What you're bringing | Per person | So a plate for 10 is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leafy salad or slaw | 100–150 g | a big bowl, not a side bowl |
| Pasta, rice or potato salad | about 100 g | 1 kg finished weight |
| Nibbles before a meal | 4–6 pieces | 50 pieces |
| Nibbles that *are* the meal | 10–12 pieces | two trays, not one |
| Dip | about 50 g | 500 g dip, 2 packets of crackers |
| Cheese on a grazing board | 30–50 g as a nibble | 400 g across 3 cheeses |
| Dessert | 1 generous piece | 12 pieces — always cut spares |
| Sausage rolls or party pies | 3–4 each | 36, and they'll go first |

If you're the host: the honest fix is a list everyone can see. The [bring a plate board](https://bitibybit.com/bring-a-plate/) lets you set categories with limits — four salads, three desserts, two sides — and share one link. Run one free, no signup, and the doubling-up sorts itself out.

## If there's no oven (or no room in it)

Assume this by default. On the day, the host's oven is holding a roast and the stovetop has four things on it. Bring something that is finished before you leave home.

- Any salad — slaw, potato, pasta, rice, roast pumpkin and rocket, tabouli, three-bean.
- A grazing board: cheese, cured meat, olives, dried fruit, crackers.
- Dips and things to dip: hummus, tzatziki, beetroot dip, carrot and cucumber sticks.
- Cold sausage rolls or party pies — they're perfectly good at room temperature, and everyone eats them anyway.
- Frittata or quiche, cooked the night before, served cold in squares.
- Rice paper rolls, sushi, sandwiches cut small.
- Dessert that lives in the fridge: cheesecake, tiramisu, trifle, chocolate slice, lemon slice.
- A fruit platter. Underrated, always eaten, no washing up.

## If it has to survive a hot car

An Australian car in summer is a food-safety problem with wheels. The widely used local guideline for ready-to-eat food sitting between 5°C and 60°C is the 2-hour / 4-hour rule: under two hours, it can go back in the fridge; two to four hours, use it now; past four hours, bin it. Boot temperature counts toward that clock.

Travels well:

anything vinegar or oil dressed,
 anything baked and dry, whole fruit, hard cheese, cooked pastry, chocolate
 slices in a cool box.

Travels badly:

cream, custard, mayonnaise-heavy potato salad,
 seafood, soft cheese, meringue in humidity, anything with a glaze that
 melts. Bring these in an esky with a frozen bottle of water, or bring
 something else.

Practical fixes: freeze a bottle of water and pack it against the dish; put the esky on the floor behind a seat rather than in the boot; dress the salad on arrival; carry the pav base flat and whip the cream at the other end.

## If it has to feed twenty, cheaply

The trick isn't a cheap recipe, it's a cheap base plus one strong flavour. Cost lives in the meat, cheese and nuts; the base is nearly free.

- **Pasta salad** — 1 kg dry pasta, tin of corn, capsicum, spring onion, a decent dressing.
- **Rice salad** — the church hall classic. Rice, peas, corn, curry powder, sultanas if you're brave.
- **Coleslaw** — two cabbages feed a crowd for pocket change.
- **Curried sausages, dhal, or a big chilli** with rice, if there's somewhere to keep it warm.
- **Baked potatoes with toppings**, or a tray of potato bake.
- **Chocolate crackles, jelly slice, or a tray of brownie** cut small — thirty pieces out of one tin.
- **Watermelon.** One melon, forty pieces, gone in ten minutes.

## If nobody wants to reheat anything

Room-temperature food is the whole point of a plate. Salads, slices, sandwiches, boards, cold roast chicken, marinated vegetables, scones with jam and cream assembled on site. If your dish only works hot, ask the host first — and bring it in something that can go straight to the table, not in a saucepan that needs a burner.

## If there'll be kids

Kids eat plain food in small pieces. Cocktail frankfurts, cheerios, sausage rolls, cheese cubes, cut fruit, plain crackers, carrot sticks, fairy bread, mini muffins, popcorn. Two things worth knowing: whole grapes, nuts, popcorn and hard lollies are choking risks for the under-fours, so cut grapes lengthways and keep the nuts at adult height; and a plate of plain food gets eaten by nervous adults too.

## If someone has an allergy

Ask the host before you cook — allergies are the one part of a potluck that isn't a matter of taste. Then **label your dish with what's in it**: a strip of masking tape reading "contains egg, wheat, walnuts" does more good than any dietary guess. If you're catering deliberately around an allergy, cross-contamination is the hard part: separate boards, clean utensils, and a serving spoon that stays with your dish so the peanut-satay spoon never lands in the fruit salad. Coeliac disease and true nut allergy aren't preferences, and "just a little bit" isn't a plan.

Easy allergy-friendly options: fruit platter, plain rice salad, roast vegetables, olives and pickles, corn chips with a tomato salsa, meringue-free fruit and coconut slice, plain grilled chicken skewers.

## The forty-minute rescue

Honestly? The servo won't save you. Petrol station shelves are pies, chips and lollies, and everyone can tell. If you've got any choice at all, give a supermarket seven minutes instead:

- A tub of good hummus, a tub of tzatziki, a bag of Turkish bread, a punnet of cherry tomatoes. Arrange on a board. Looks deliberate.
- Bagged slaw kit plus a tin of chickpeas — a real salad in four minutes.
- A hot barbecue chook and a bag of rolls. Never refused.
- Cheese, quince paste, crackers, grapes, a handful of dried apricots.
- A watermelon and a sharp knife.
- Two blocks of good chocolate and a punnet of strawberries.

And if you genuinely only have the servo: a bag of ice is a real contribution, and the host will mean it when they thank you.

## Getting it there in one piece

- **Bring your own serving spoon.** The single most forgotten item at every gathering in the country.
- **Write your name on the base** in masking tape, or bring it in something you don't want back.
- **Foil trays and a lid** beat the good ceramic dish that then has to be tracked down and returned.
- Line the boot with a towel, or wedge the dish in a laundry basket so it can't slide.
- Take dressings, cream and toppings separately in a jar.
- If it's hot, esky. If it's meant to be warm, wrap in a towel — it'll hold for the drive across town.

## Plate etiquette, briefly

Tell the host what you're bringing so the spread balances. Turn up with it ready to eat. Don't hijack the kitchen. Take your dish home at the end unless you've been told to leave the leftovers. If you can't cook, say so and bring drinks, ice, bread or a fruit platter — nobody's marking you. And if you're the one hosting, put the list up early: the [bring a plate board](https://bitibybit.com/bring-a-plate/) takes about a minute, is free, needs no signup from you or your guests, and stops the annual pavlova surplus before it starts.

## FAQ

### What does "bring a plate" mean in Australia?

It means bring a dish of food to share with the whole group, not an empty plate and not a serving for yourself. It's the standard wording on invitations to barbecues, school events, wakes and street parties in Australia and New Zealand.

### How much food should I bring?

Enough for eight to ten people as one dish among several. For a salad that's about 100–150 g per person; for nibbles, four to six pieces each before a meal, or ten to twelve if the nibbles are the meal.

### What's the easiest plate to bring?

A grazing board, a bagged slaw dressed at home, a fruit platter, or a tray of slice cut into squares. All are finished before you leave, need no oven, and travel without incident.

### What should I bring if I can't cook?

Cheese and crackers, good dip and vegetable sticks, a hot barbecue chicken, a watermelon, bread rolls, drinks or a bag of ice. Every one of them gets eaten, and none of them require a recipe.

### Do I take my dish home?

Yes — take the dish and its serving spoon home unless the host has said to leave the leftovers. Bringing something disposable saves everyone the follow-up text message.
